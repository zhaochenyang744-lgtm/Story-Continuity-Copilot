# Chapter 1 The Weight of Water

Maren Voss watched the last strip of daylight disappear behind the launch cradle. Below them, Nacre Reach was already dark. The drowned settlement had lain under the reservoir for thirty years, but people on shore still described its streets as if they could walk home after supper. Maren had agreed to survey the old meeting hall. She had not agreed to bring back everything the water had kept.

"Latch, your sleeve," said Ivo Pell.

Latch was Maren's working nickname, earned when she had repaired a jammed cargo lock with a spoon. Her sleeve was caught under the edge of the console. She freed it and thanked him. Ivo was called Finch by the crew because he whistled while checking equipment, although he had stopped whistling as soon as the cradle began to move.

Sela Trent sat behind them, holding the mission sheet flat against her knees. Sela was still a trainee pilot and was not authorized to operate the submersible alone. Orrin Beck would supervise her at the controls. He let her read the launch sequence aloud without finishing any of her sentences, even when she paused at a word she knew perfectly well.

Maren kept the amber survey slate beside her. The amber survey slate belonged to the survey team and was currently in Maren's custody. Its screen displayed only shades of grey; the hardware could not show colour. She checked the numbered marks instead of relying on the differently coloured tabs someone had attached to its case.

Halfway down, three evenly spaced knocks travelled through the hull. Sela looked at Orrin. He checked the pressure readings before answering her unspoken question. Nothing on the panel explained the sound. Maren noted the depth and asked everyone to keep still. The knocks came again, then ceased when the cradle cable shifted.

"We have an observation," Orrin said. "We do not yet have a visitor."

At the bottom, the lamps found a row of windows filled with silt. Ivo leaned forward as if someone might appear behind the glass. Maren asked him to record what the light actually reached. He lowered his gaze to the page. Outside, their own beams moved over a wall on which a faded line still showed where people had once measured the height of children. The mission suddenly felt heavier than the vehicle carrying it.

# Chapter 2 The Door Between Pressures

The Rill settled on a level patch beside the meeting hall. Sela made a small correction, waited for Orrin's nod, and stopped the thrusters. The silt moved past the windows in slow sheets. Maren resisted the urge to begin naming rooms before she could see their outlines. A map drawn before the flood was useful, but it was not a promise that every wall remained where it had been.

Orrin checked the transfer chamber. The Rill's outer hatch could open only when the pressure on both sides was equal. Its mechanical interlock had no manual bypass. Ivo asked whether the waiting time could be shortened. Orrin pointed to the gauges rather than giving him a lecture, and Ivo watched the two readings move towards each other.

In the equipment tray lay the coil beacon. Orrin had custody of the coil beacon and used it to mark a safe return point. He tested its pulse, labelled the reading, and put it back into its padded cradle. Sela asked to repeat the test. He allowed her to do so, then made her explain what a failed result would have changed about their plan.

Through the window, Maren could see a small protected case fixed to the hall wall. It contained the copper tally ring, an object catalogued before the reservoir was filled. The copper tally ring had been made with four radial grooves. It was not on their recovery list. She marked its position without instructing anyone to touch the case.

The hall entrance had collapsed, leaving a narrow gap under a beam. Ivo suggested sending the small camera through. Sela prepared the line while Maren read the old room numbers from the plan. The camera showed chairs lying on their sides, a heap of roof tiles, and something pale under a table. Nobody could tell whether it was cloth or a mineral deposit.

"Then we call it something pale," Maren said.

Ivo wrote the description with visible reluctance. On the return ascent he asked whether the report would look foolish with so many uncertain words. Maren looked at the water folding over the lamps. A confident wrong word, she told him, could send somebody's hand towards the wrong object on the next dive. He crossed out the question mark after pale and wrote the limits of the camera view beneath it instead.

# Chapter 3 A Voice Without a Seat

On shore, the recovered camera footage looked less dramatic than it had underwater. The pale shape occupied only a few blurred frames. Sela stopped the recording, moved back, and played it again. Ivo stood behind her chair with a cup he had forgotten to drink. Each replay made him more certain he could see a folded sleeve. Maren asked him to cover the label he had written and describe the shape once more.

This time he said it might be a sheet. Then he laughed at himself and returned to the description they had agreed upon. Orrin opened the equipment case and began checking the camera line. He worked slowly, moving every section through his fingers, as if the line were a story whose missing sentence could be felt rather than read.

Maren handed the amber survey slate to Ivo for continuing custody. Ivo accepted it, checked the stored records, and placed it in his own padded shoulder case. Maren retained a paper summary. She told him that keeping the slate meant protecting earlier observations as well as adding new ones. He moved his cold cup away from the case before replying.

During the audio review, a faint voice appeared on the unused second channel. It spoke a sequence of numbers, too softly for all of them to agree on every digit. Sela checked the crew log. None of the four had used that channel during the dive. They could not identify the speaker or the source of the transmission.

"Could it be an old recording?" Ivo asked.

"It could," Maren answered. "That is still a question."

They kept the original audio untouched and made separate notes of what each person thought they heard. Orrin refused to let them combine uncertain digits into a single neat sequence. A neat sequence would be easier to search, but it might never have existed outside their table.

By evening, Sela had completed the practice manoeuvres required before her final pilot assessment. She stayed beside the moored Rill after the others had gone inside. The vehicle moved a little against its lines, and the launch cradle creaked. She listened for the three knocks, heard only ordinary metal settling, and went to supper without deciding that the mystery had solved itself merely because the shore was quiet.

# Chapter 4 Permission to Hold the Controls

Sela's assessment began in shallow water, close enough to shore for her to see boots moving along the landing stage. An examiner sat in Orrin's usual seat. He gave her a route, changed one waypoint, and asked her to explain what she would do if the return marker failed. She spoke too quickly at first, stopped, and began again. The examiner waited without rescuing her from the silence.

When the assessment ended, Sela received full pilot certification. She was now authorized to operate the Rill independently. Orrin met her at the cradle with a towel and asked whether she wanted to pilot the afternoon survey. She had expected congratulations before a question about work. Then she understood that he was offering her the actual consequence of the certificate.

"Yes," she said. "And I want you to check my route."

Orrin smiled and took the route sheet. Independence had not made a second pair of eyes unnecessary.

They ate lunch on the quay. Orrin mentioned his first job at sea, describing how he had spent an entire watch searching for a loose spoon inside a locker. He never gave the name of that first vessel. Ivo asked whether he had found the spoon. Orrin said he had found three, which was not the same as discovering which one had made the noise.

On the afternoon dive, Sela held the Rill steady beside the hall while Maren directed the camera towards a side entrance. The beam overhead appeared intact, although its fastenings needed closer inspection. They did not go beneath it. Ivo used the slate to mark the limits of the view and left a deliberate blank where the camera had not reached.

The three knocks returned during the ascent. Sela recorded the depth without changing course abruptly. Orrin listened and asked her to compare the sound with the movement of the cradle cable. This time she could feel a slight tug just before the first knock. They had a possible relation to investigate, not yet a cause.

Back at the quay, she secured the vehicle and signed the pilot entry herself. Maren checked the time, Ivo secured the slate, and Orrin brought the cable inspection kit. Sela folded her certificate into a dry envelope. It mattered to her, but the small steady signature in the daily log felt more like the beginning of the person she hoped to become.

# Chapter 5 The Small Metal Answer

The loose part was no larger than Orrin's thumbnail. He found it inside the cradle coupling, where a worn retaining jaw could strike the housing as the cable changed tension. Maren stood beside him while he moved the assembly by hand. Three knocks travelled along the metal, familiar enough to make Sela turn from the far end of the quay.

They tested the coupling under controlled load before changing anything. The three knocks came from the worn retaining jaw in the launch cradle coupling. They were not knocks from inside the flooded hall. Ivo entered the result on the slate and attached the inspection reference. He looked almost disappointed, then admitted that a mechanical cause was a better companion on the next descent than an imagined person outside the hull.

Orrin replaced the worn part and repeated the test. The coupling moved without the extra strikes. Sela listened twice, not because she distrusted the repair, but because she wanted to learn the ordinary sound left behind. Maren put the removed jaw in a labelled maintenance pouch. It would be kept as evidence of the repair rather than turned into a souvenir of their fear.

That afternoon a woman from the former settlement visited the quay. She had heard they were surveying the hall and brought a photograph of a cabinet once used for community records. The photograph showed no measurements, only the cabinet behind a row of smiling children. Maren asked permission to copy it. The woman agreed, provided they did not crop the children out merely because furniture was what interested them.

Ivo found himself studying one child's hand resting against the cabinet. He could not know whose hand it was from the photograph alone. He asked the woman, who remembered the child but preferred to write the name privately on the back. Maren accepted that boundary and kept the public working copy without it.

Before supper, they compared the photograph with the camera footage. A dark rectangle in the side room might be the cabinet. Might was still doing honest work. Sela proposed a route that would keep the vehicle clear of the unstable entrance while allowing the small camera another angle. Orrin checked the distances, and Maren agreed to inspect from outside first. The reservoir held a room that people remembered. Their job was to approach the room carefully enough that remembering did not become an excuse to damage it.

# Chapter 6 Waiting Above the Hall

Wind delayed the next launch. The reservoir was sheltered, but the cradle swung far enough to make Orrin refuse the lift. Ivo looked at the calm water beyond the quay and began to protest. Sela pointed to the suspended vehicle rather than the distant surface. He followed her gaze and saw the small sideways movement that would become a hard impact if they pretended it did not matter.

They spent the morning sorting records. Maren was serving as acting survey director; the permanent appointment had not yet been made. A letter from the project board asked for an interim report. She read it at the end of the table, then put it aside long enough to check Ivo's labels. The title might change later. The responsibility for today's uncertain observations was already hers.

Orrin prepared the coil beacon for the proposed return point outside the side entrance. A loose corner of the checklist caught beneath his elbow, and he stopped to free it. Sela checked the route against the vehicle's turning space, while Ivo compared the cabinet photograph with the most recent footage. No one treated the photograph as proof that its contents had survived beneath the water.

At noon, the wind eased. They launched only after repeating the cradle checks. Sela brought the Rill to a position above the hall, keeping the lamps away from the unstable main entrance. Through the side window they could see the narrow passage they hoped to inspect on the next operation. The beam did not move, but that alone did not establish its condition.

"Today we measure from here," Maren said. "We do not borrow tomorrow's permission."

They gathered the exterior measurements and returned to shore without entering the side chamber. The planned first interior inspection had not yet happened. Ivo recorded that plainly, though he disliked how little it resembled the ambitious plan pinned above his desk.

That evening the four gathered beside the secured Rill with the route sheet open, ready to inspect the side chamber on the following operation if conditions allowed. The second-channel voice remained unidentified. Maren included it in the unresolved notes, separate from the repaired knocking fault. Sela ran her finger along the proposed route once more. The line ended at a place they had not reached, and for the first time she found that ending reassuring. The paper knew where their knowledge stopped.

# Chapter 7 A Room Still Holding Its Shape

The small inspection unit entered the side chamber before anyone considered taking the Rill closer. Ivo paid out the camera line in measured lengths while Sela held the vehicle steady. Orrin watched the return marker. Maren watched the image. For several minutes the screen showed only floating particles, then the camera turned and the cabinet appeared with one door hanging open.

The team completed its first interior camera inspection of the side chamber that morning. The inspection was not cancelled or abandoned halfway through. They recorded the cabinet, the surrounding floor, and the supports visible from the safe camera route. What lay inside the deepest drawer remained outside the camera's view, and Maren refused to describe it as empty.

"A room can be inspected without every corner being known," she told Ivo when he asked how to label the result.

The cabinet had settled against the wall rather than falling. Several sealed containers remained on its lower shelf. Their labels were unreadable through the silt. Sela suggested approaching from a little farther left, and the new angle showed a crack in the floor below. That observation changed the recovery plan before any lifting equipment entered the room.

They withdrew the camera carefully. A loop in the line caught briefly against the edge of a chair, and Ivo stopped rather than pulling harder. Orrin guided him through releasing the tension and changing the angle. The line came free. Nobody praised the snag, but Maren thanked him for reporting it immediately instead of trying to hide it with a quick tug.

On shore, the visitor's photograph was laid beside the new images. The cabinet was recognizable, though the room around it had altered. Maren sent a factual progress note to the project office through the ordinary reporting process, stating what had been inspected and what would require further authorization. The team did not promise the containers would be recovered intact.

Ivo stayed late to check the image numbers. Sela brought him a plate from supper and sat without asking whether the work was almost done. He showed her the chair that had caught the line. In the still image it looked harmless, almost welcoming. She studied it for a while, then asked him to include the angle from which the danger had become visible. One photograph, she said, could make a room look kinder than it was.

# Chapter 8 A Different Pair of Hands

The recovery authorization specified a limited operation: document and retrieve two sealed containers, leaving the cabinet itself in place. Maren read the terms aloud at the morning briefing. Ivo asked whether a third could be taken if it proved easy to reach. She answered that ease was not authorization. They could report a new opportunity without quietly turning it into a different mission.

Orrin transferred custody of the coil beacon to Sela. She accepted it, checked its serial mark and pulse, and packed it in her own equipment case. Orrin kept a copy of the maintenance history. From then on, Sela was responsible for its issue and return, even when another crew member handled it during an operation.

They rehearsed the lifting sequence on the quay with empty practice containers. The first attempt was awkward. Ivo's line crossed Orrin's, and Maren discovered she could not see the lower attachment point from her planned position. Sela stopped the rehearsal and suggested moving the observation camera. Nobody had been in danger on the quay, which was precisely why they had chosen to make their mistakes there.

During the second rehearsal the lines stayed separate. Orrin asked Ivo to explain how he would signal a snag. Ivo answered, then demonstrated it with his hands. Maren could see the signal through the practice frame. They repeated the sequence once more because the camera position had changed, not because repetition itself made the operation safe.

In the afternoon, Sela took a short walk along the reservoir edge. The former road descended into the water and disappeared before reaching the hall. A boy was skipping flat stones across its submerged course. She watched one stone bounce three times and sink. For a moment she imagined the old street continuing beneath it with people still waiting at their doors. Then the boy asked whether she had a better stone, and she helped him look among the loose ones above the waterline.

Back at the workroom, she found the beacon case exactly where she had placed it. She checked it anyway and entered the result. Ivo had fallen asleep over a page of practice timings. She moved his cup away from the paper without waking him, then opened the window a little. Outside, the reservoir made no acknowledgement of their preparation. Its surface kept moving in small ordinary folds, covering both the road they remembered and the room they still had to reach.

# Chapter 9 The Letter on the Wall

The permanent appointment arrived in a plain envelope while Maren was washing sediment from a measuring frame. Her hands were wet, so she asked Ivo to leave it on the dry table. He recognized the project board's mark and hovered nearby until she told him that important paper could survive five minutes without an audience. Sela laughed from the doorway, then went back to checking the weather notice.

The board appointed Maren as permanent survey director, replacing her acting status. She read the letter once, signed the acknowledgment, and pinned a copy beside the duty list. Orrin congratulated her. She thanked him and asked whether the revised lifting arrangement was ready for review. He refused to let the practical question erase the congratulations, so she accepted both with a tired smile.

The appointment gave her no extra knowledge of the room below the reservoir. During the briefing she said so plainly. Ivo asked whether the recovery date was now certain. Maren pointed to the weather and equipment conditions still listed on the sheet. A permanent title did not make temporary conditions disappear.

They spent the afternoon reviewing the interior footage at different speeds. Sela noticed that a strip near the cabinet moved independently of the current passing the doorway. Orrin thought it might be loose fabric. Maren asked for a closer view on the next camera pass before any line crossed that area. The observation did not cancel the operation, but it added a specific question to the approach.

Ivo brought out the amber survey slate and compared the new marks with the earlier plan. Its grey display made two coloured pen annotations look alike in the scanned image. He relabelled the annotations with letters on a separate working copy, leaving the original scan unchanged. Maren watched him do it and felt more encouraged than she had by the appointment letter.

At supper, Sela asked why Maren had accepted the permanent role. Maren said she wanted to stay through the work and be answerable for its ending. She did not pretend it was the only reason she had ever wanted a steady job. Orrin passed her the bread without demanding a grander explanation.

Later, alone in the workroom, she adjusted the pinned letter so it would not cover the unresolved audio notes. The mysterious voice remained where everyone could see it. Her new title occupied a small rectangle of wall. It had not earned the right to hide a question the team still could not answer.

# Chapter 10 The Case They Left Closed

On the next descent, the lamps reached the protected wall case before they reached the side entrance. Inside lay the copper tally ring. The copper tally ring had been made with five radial grooves. Maren recorded the condition of the case from outside and kept the vehicle on the approved route. The ring was still excluded from the recovery authorization.

Ivo asked whether a photograph would be useful to the shore archive. She said yes, provided taking it did not require an unplanned approach. Sela held a safe position, and the camera obtained a clear enough image through the sediment. Nobody suggested opening the case simply because the object was small enough to carry in a pocket.

The moving strip beside the cabinet proved to be a torn length of wall covering. Orrin checked that it was not attached to either of the selected containers. The team documented its position and chose a line path that avoided it. Ivo repeated the snag signal before they began, less self-conscious now about rehearsing something everyone already knew.

They attached the lifting frame to the first container and raised it only a little. Sediment drifted from beneath its base. Maren watched the cabinet and floor for movement while Sela maintained the vehicle's position. The container came free without the shelf shifting. They secured it in the transfer frame, then paused to review the readings before approaching the second.

"We have done one," Maren said when Ivo reached for the next line. "That does not mean we have already done two."

The second container was partly obstructed by a fallen board. Moving the board would exceed the agreed method. They left it in place, recorded the obstruction, and returned with only the first container. Ivo looked disappointed but did not describe the result as a complete failure. The authorized number was a ceiling, not a reason to force the room to cooperate.

On shore, the container went into a controlled holding bath under the conservation team's direction. The survey crew did not open it to satisfy their own curiosity. Sela checked the beacon back into her case, and Ivo secured the slate. Maren wrote the day's result carefully: one retrieved, one left pending. Beyond the quay, the reservoir darkened under a bank of cloud. It still held more than they had brought back, and for once that simple fact did not sound like an accusation.

# Chapter 11 What the Water Had Not Promised

The conservation room smelled of clean water and wet stone. Maren stood at the marked boundary while a conservator inspected the retrieved container. Ivo had expected the lid to come off immediately. Instead, the first hour was spent measuring the container and documenting its surface. Sela leaned against the wall, careful not to let her coat touch the working table.

Orrin asked what the survey crew could usefully provide. The conservator requested the lifting angles, the storage conditions before recovery, and every image showing the lower seam. Ivo opened the amber survey slate and found the references. His earlier insistence on image numbers suddenly seemed less like tidy administration and more like part of the object's care.

They were told that opening might take time and that some contents could have deteriorated beyond recognition. Maren passed this information to the project office without softening it into an encouraging promise. The photograph of the old cabinet remained on her desk, children and all. It showed what had once existed, not a guarantee of what the water would return.

At lunch the visitor came back to ask about progress. Maren explained the single recovery and the obstruction around the second container. The woman listened, then asked whether the cabinet was still standing. Maren showed her the latest safe view. The woman touched the edge of the copy, avoiding the image itself, and said that was more than she had expected to learn this week.

Ivo later admitted he had imagined she would be angry that they had not brought everything back. Sela asked whether she had ever said she wanted everything. He thought about the photograph and shook his head. The crew had been carrying some expectations nobody had actually handed them.

Before closing the workroom, they reviewed the second-channel audio again. No new source had been identified. Orrin checked the equipment log and found no entry that explained the transmission. They preserved the uncertainty instead of attaching it to the recovered container simply because both were interesting.

Maren left the appointment letter on the wall and gathered the next day's papers. The remaining work included an obstruction review, an equipment check, and a conversation with the conservation staff. None sounded like the end of a great adventure. She found that comforting. The settlement below Nacre Reach deserved people willing to remain after the exciting part had become a list of precise, patient obligations.

# Chapter 12 The Table Above the Reservoir

Morning light reached the workroom before anyone turned on the lamps. Sela arrived with the beacon case, checked its latch, and placed it in the assigned locker. Someone had left a chair blocking the door. She moved it to the table and found a damp mark on the seat, so she fetched a cloth before the clerk could sit down. Ivo came in carrying breakfast on a tray. He waited for her to finish, then put the tray between the empty places.

Maren read the conservation update aloud. The retrieved container remained under controlled examination, and the team had no confirmed account of its contents. The second container still waited in the hall. A revised recovery method would require review before another attempt. Ivo entered those limits without trying to make the page sound more complete than the work.

Sela prepared the next route review while Orrin checked the maintenance records. She put two possible approaches on separate sheets and asked him where the camera line might catch. He held one page towards the window, following the narrow turn with a finger. They disagreed about the distance until Ivo brought over the measured view. Sela crossed out her estimate, leaving enough room for the actual figure to be read without guessing which mark it replaced.

Maren signed the planning page and arranged a conversation about the revised recovery method. The clerk asked for a second copy, and she searched beneath the breakfast tray before remembering she had left it by the window. A crumb had settled on the signature line. She brushed it into her palm, checked that the ink had dried, and handed the sheet across. The clerk thanked her and began matching the numbered attachments to the list.

The second-channel voice was still unidentified. The crew did not know who had spoken the numbers or why the transmission had appeared there. The knocking fault, by contrast, had been explained and repaired at the cradle coupling. Sela put the two records in their proper sections instead of leaving both under a dramatic heading about strange sounds.

The four finished the morning by gathering around the table with the inventory sheets open, preparing to reconcile the recovered item, the remaining equipment, and the next review requirements. Outside, the launch cradle moved slightly in the breeze. Ivo looked up at the sound, recognized the ordinary creak, and returned to his page. The water still concealed a street, a room, and many things they could not name. On this side of it, they had made a table where known things could stay known, and unanswered questions did not have to disguise themselves as conclusions.
